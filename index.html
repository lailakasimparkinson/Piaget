Jean Piaget and Learning Technology

This site introduces Jean Piaget's theory of cognitive development to fellow students of educational technology. It begins with one of his best-known experiments, moves through his central concepts and four stages of thinking, and then considers what his ideas might imply for the design of learning technologies, and where they fall short.

Begin with a puzzle

To understand is to invent.

Jean Piaget, title of his 1973 book

The puzzle

Piaget's conservation task is a convenient place to begin. It belongs to a widely replicated class of findings in developmental psychology, and it shows quickly why he came to think that children's reasoning differs in kind from that of adults. Two glasses hold the same amount of juice, and one is poured into a taller, thinner glass. Because nothing has been added or taken away, an adult sees no reason to doubt that the amount remains the same, yet a younger child frequently does. Four ideas to hold onto

Piaget (1952) treated children as active participants in their own development. On this view, understanding is constructed through acting on the world, testing it, and revising what one believes about it. Four terms are central to the account.

Schema
A mental framework for organizing what one knows about something. An infant's grasping schema, for example, may become a toddler's "bird" schema, and later an adult's concept of an ecosystem.
Assimilation
Taking in new experience through an existing schema. A child who knows "dog" meets an unfamiliar breed and files it under the same category, leaving the schema as it was.
Accommodation
Changing a schema because an experience does not fit it. The same child meets a goat, is told that it is not a dog, and has to build a new category.
Equilibration
The process of balancing assimilation and accommodation that, for Piaget, drives development. A mismatch between experience and understanding produces discomfort, adaptation resolves it, and the resulting balance is more capable than the one before.

On Piaget's account, development is driven largely by a mismatch between what a learner expects and what they encounter. Cognitive conflict can support learning, although instruction, practice, feedback, motivation and social interaction all contribute as well. This suggests that the practical question for design is which mismatch a particular learner is able to resolve.

To test the terms, classify what happens to a toddler in each of three moments.

A note for designers

If disequilibrium contributes to learning, then it is something a designer can shape. A tool that never surprises its learners gives them little to accommodate, while one that surprises them too often, or too much, may leave them unable to make sense of what they are seeing.

Piaget in learning technology

The connection between Piaget and learning technology runs largely through Seymour Papert, who worked with him in Geneva from 1958 to 1963. Papert later asked what computers might become for children if learning is understood as construction, and his answer has shaped a good part of the field.

Constructionism retains Piaget's view that learners build their own knowledge, and adds the claim that this happens especially well when they are building something shareable in the world (Papert & Harel, 1991). Block-based coding, maker spaces and a good deal of educational game design draw on this idea.

A further point comes from constructivist technology studies. Pinch and Bijker (1984) argue that a technology's design reflects the interpretations of the social groups involved in making and using it, along with whatever engineering principles apply. Applied to learning technology, this suggests that any tool carries assumptions about what learning is and who is doing it, whether or not its designers name a theory. Reading a tool through Piaget is one way of making those assumptions visible, and it is the approach I take with the three tools below.

Three tools, read through a Piagetian lens

For each tool I ask two questions. What does it assume about how learning works, and where does Piaget's theory stop being able to explain it?

Five design principles that follow from this

  • Productive conflict.Asking learners to predict before they see a result, and then showing them where the prediction failed, gives them a mismatch to work on. Novices usually need support in making sense of that conflict, so it should not be left to unguided discovery (Kirschner et al., 2006).
  • Action on objects.Giving learners things to manipulate, whether physical or virtual, follows from Piaget's emphasis on action as a source of understanding.
  • Representation matched to readiness.Tasks can begin with concrete representations and move gradually toward abstraction as understanding firms up.
  • Errors as information.Piaget's clinical method asked children why they gave a particular answer. Feedback that exposes a learner's reasoning is therefore likely to be more useful than feedback that only marks an answer right or wrong.
  • The limits of individual construction.Open exploration can be paired with social support, and the next section considers how the two fit together.

Where the theory strains

Later research has tended to support Piaget's observations more than some of his explanations. Four criticisms seem especially relevant for anyone applying his work to the design of learning technologies.

Infants may know more than Piaget credited

Looking-time studies suggest that infants as young as five months react to physically impossible events, such as a solid object seeming to pass through another (Baillargeon et al., 1985). Researchers continue to debate what this reaction means, although many read it as early knowledge that Piaget's search tasks were unable to detect.

Task design affects what children can show

In one well-known study, children conserved more often when a "naughty teddy" disrupted the display accidentally than when an adult rearranged it deliberately (McGarrigle & Donaldson, 1974). Donaldson (1978) used findings like this to argue that some apparent failures reflect how a task is worded and framed, and not an absence of the underlying logic. For designers, this means that instructions and context form part of the cognitive demand placed on a learner.

The stages are less tidy than they appear

Children often succeed on one task and fail a structurally similar one, an unevenness that Piaget called décalage. Later work also qualified the idea that formal operations are a universal, general endpoint, and Piaget (1972) himself suggested that they may appear mainly in fields where a person has expertise.

The role of other people

Piaget's account tends to picture the child as working largely alone, much like a scientist at a bench. Vygotsky (1978) argued instead that thinking develops through interaction with more capable others and through language. Of the four criticisms, this one has the most direct consequences for learning technology.

Using them together

In practice the two can be treated as layers of a single design, and a physics simulation offers a convenient example.

The Piagetian layer

  • The learner predicts what will happen
  • They change the variables and watch the result
  • A wrong prediction creates the conflict

The Vygotskian layer

  • Prompts that ask them to explain the surprise
  • Worked examples for novices
  • Peer discussion of the result
  • Adaptive hints that fade as they improve, pitched just beyond what they can do alone

The Piagetian layer gives the learner something to work on, and the Vygotskian layer helps the learner make sense of the result. Kirschner et al. (2006) raise their concern about minimal guidance at this second point, since a novice who meets a prediction error without support may not know how to interpret it.
